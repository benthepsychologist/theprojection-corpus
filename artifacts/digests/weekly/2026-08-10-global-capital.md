## The week's throughline

This week is the one where AI-buildout financing stopped being an equity
narrative and became a capital-markets risk category with its own price
tags, warnings, and a named skeptic. It opened with Nvidia turning itself
from chip vendor into lender of last resort — MOUs with six of the
largest asset managers (Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield,
Goldman Sachs, KKR) to mobilize over $500 billion in third-party capital,
treating GPU capacity as collateral rather than depreciating hardware
(Monday). Every day after added a harder number to the same question:
CoreWeave's Q2 earnings showed backlog growth and financing cost climbing
together for the first time in one borrower's own numbers (Tuesday);
Lambda priced a circular, Nvidia-linked loan and picked up the first
investment-grade rating a private neocloud has ever carried (Wednesday);
AMD raised its largest-ever bond sale explicitly to fund its own $5
billion Anthropic stake, moving the borrowing up from the neocloud layer
to the chipmaker layer for the first time, and a Jane Street-backed
data-center bond priced below investment grade — this map's first
sub-investment-grade print on the whole AI-buildout-debt thread
(Thursday). By Thursday the skepticism turned specific and named: Moody's
warned banks are building concentrated operational dependence on a
handful of AI vendors, a Jefferies note quantified the four largest
hyperscalers' combined free cash flow collapsing from $60B (Q4 2025) to
$7B (Q2 2026) against $165B of quarterly AI capex, and Michael Burry
named Nvidia's own $500B platform directly, calling its credit structures
"abnormal" and its revenue "largely financed through circular
arrangements." Running underneath all of it, all week: Iran's war kept
escalating on the ground — Trump's "total control" claim over the Strait
of Hormuz, Iran's flat rejection of it, a second Houthi strike on the
same Saudi refinery, and the first drone hits on UAE-flagged tankers —
while oil itself fell on OPEC's fourth straight demand-growth downgrade
and a huge US inventory build, geopolitical rhetoric and price moving in
opposite directions on repeated, separate tests. On the rates side, July
CPI cooled enough to cut the market's implied odds of a September hike
roughly in half (48% to ~30% by week's end), even as the US Treasury paid
the highest 30-year borrowing rate since 2001 — near-term rate risk
easing while the government's own long-run cost of capital got harder.
Two long-tracked expectations resolved cleanly and on schedule this week:
CoreWeave's Q2 print, and — the week's closing item — Berkshire's Q2 13F,
read straight off SEC EDGAR rather than any outlet's framing, showing the
Alphabet stake nearly doubling in both share count and value while the
rest of the portfolio rotated toward housing and away from a handful of
consumer names.

## By radar question

### Q7 — Where is capital and economic power concentrating, in my markets and above them?

**What moved, in order.** The vendor-financing structure this lens has
tracked since late July produced its first genuinely public, named
skepticism this week, not just a spread or a CDS move. Nvidia's $500B
compute-financing-platform MOUs (Monday) set the week's frame, and by
Thursday three independent voices had converged on the same worry from
three different angles: Moody's flagged banks' own operational dependence
on a small set of AI vendors as a systemic-cascade risk; Jefferies put a
hard number on hyperscaler cash-flow compression ($60B to $7B in two
quarters, against $165B of quarterly capex); and Michael Burry, for the
first time, named Nvidia's actual platform rather than the AI boom in
general, calling its structures "abnormal" and its revenue "largely
financed through circular arrangements." In between, the transaction
layer kept confirming the mechanism is real, not theoretical: CoreWeave's
Q2 print showed net interest expense more than doubling year-over-year
right alongside its backlog growth; Lambda's loan showed the exact
circular shape (Nvidia as investor, supplier, customer, and counterparty
at once) that the Bank for International Settlements warned about in
June, and picked up the first investment-grade stamp a private neocloud
has ever carried on that shape of deal; AMD's bond sale moved the
borrowing up a layer, from neoclouds to the chipmaker itself; and a Jane
Street-backed data-center bond became this map's first sub-investment-
grade print in the whole thread. Credit markets themselves haven't priced
any of this as stress yet — high-yield and investment-grade OAS spreads
sat essentially flat all week (2.70-2.72 and 0.78-0.79), and the VIX
actually drifted down from 15.46 to 14.63 — so the skepticism is running
ahead of the pricing, not confirmed by it. On the macro backdrop: Brent
crude round-tripped from ~$88 to $92.54 (Tuesday, a second straight ~5-6%
day) back down to $87-88 by week's end, moving opposite to the week's
escalating Iran rhetoric on every test — OPEC's fourth straight
demand-growth downgrade, a 17.4M-barrel US inventory build, and repeated
Houthi/drone strikes all landed without a sustained price response, the
clearest evidence yet that this market is pricing physical fundamentals
over headline risk. July CPI cooled to 3.4% (Tuesday), cutting the
market's implied September-hike odds from 48% toward ~38-42% same-day and
down further to ~30% by Thursday — a real, if partial, reprieve for the
side of the Fed's 9-3 hawkish-hold split that's been arguing the economy
is cooling on its own — while the Treasury's own 30-year auction cleared
at 5.216%, the highest since 2001 and the third 2026 auction of that
maturity above 5%, a structural borrowing-cost story distinct from, but
compounding, the Fed's own debate. On wealth and power: the week closed
with Berkshire's Q2 13F, read directly from EDGAR's XML holdings table —
the Alphabet position grew from 57.8M to 106.0M combined shares (+83%)
and from $16.6B to $37.8B in value (+127%), with no other AI-adjacent
equity name anywhere in the 29-issuer portfolio, sitting alongside a real
rotation elsewhere (Constellation Brands exited entirely, Bank of
America/Capital One/Kroger trimmed, Delta grown sharply, a token new D.R.
Horton position) — Buffett's own AI-capex conviction concentrated
further, unaccompanied by any broader AI bet.

**Working-note candidate, updated.** The mechanism this note has tracked
since 08-04 (hyperscaler guarantees, then a debt-vs-equity split visible
on single balance sheets, then a quantified loss number at SoftBank) gets
its sharpest extension yet this week: the risk finally has a *named
critic* attached to a *specific structure* (Burry vs. Nvidia's platform
by name, not "the AI boom" in general), plus a hard cash-flow-compression
number spanning all four hyperscalers at once, and the borrowing itself
has now been observed at three distinct layers in one week — neocloud
(CoreWeave, Lambda), chipmaker (AMD), and landlord (the Jane Street
data-center bond). None of it has moved credit spreads or the VIX yet,
which is itself the thing to track: either the skepticism is early and
credit catches up, or the credit market is right that this is still
contained. Track next: whether Burry's specific critique of Nvidia's
platform gets picked up by any rating agency or index provider (a
credibility test distinct from press coverage); whether the
sub-investment-grade Jane Street print is a one-off or the start of a
tranche pattern in AI-datacenter ABS; and whether the Fed's September
odds keep falling toward the dissent bloc's disadvantage as more August
data lands before the 09-16 decision.
