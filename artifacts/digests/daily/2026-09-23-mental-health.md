## Research & evidence

- **A University of Illinois Chicago trial of "Coach Lumen," a
  voice-based AI coach delivering problem-solving therapy, posted results
  on ClinicalTrials.gov on 09-23: at 18 weeks, participants coached by the
  AI improved more than a delayed-treatment control, and those coached
  by a human improved somewhat more than the AI-coached group on most
  measures, in unadjusted registry means.** On the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale's distress
  total, the mean change was −5.85 for the AI-coached arm (97 completers),
  −7.33 for human coaching (48) and −2.76 for the delayed-treatment
  control (49); the session-level PHQ-9 and GAD-7 changes were −4.56,
  −5.81 and −1.63, and −4.53, −5.60 and −0.80, in the same order. Three
  cautions from the record itself: the registry posts descriptive means
  only, with no between-group tests or confidence intervals; the trial's
  pre-specified primary outcome was change in a functional-MRI activation
  measure, on which the arms are not visibly different, with the symptom
  scales all listed as secondary; and the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 figures come
  from smaller session-level samples (72, 43 and 30). The 200-person Phase
  2 ran 2023 to April 2025 and is NIMH-funded with Penn State, Washington
  University and Stanford as collaborators; whether a journal paper exists
  was not checked. Academic, with a human comparator and a delayed-treatment
  control, it is the kind of evidence this thread asks whether the field can
  produce.
  ([ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05603923](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05603923))
  <!-- k: t=ai-therapy-evidence axis=research-and-evidence -->
- **OpenAI released MentalHealthBench, an open benchmark for how AI systems handle realistic mental-health conversations, built with more than 80 licensed mental-health experts from more than 20 countries** — it scores responses against expert-written rubrics across non-acute, high-acuity and emergency scenarios and adult, teen, caregiver and clinician personas, on safety, seeking context, preserving user agency and actionable guidance. In OpenAI's own paper, OpenAI's newest model, GPT-6 Astra, scored highest at 57.3 (scored as a share of possible rubric points), ahead of Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 at 52.4 and Google's Gemini 3.1 Pro at 32.1; the older GPT-4o scored 32.1. Every author is at OpenAI and the grading is done by OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol, so this is a self-authored, self-graded result rather than an independent ranking; the paper names seeking appropriate context and calibrating urgency as the models' main weaknesses. ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-mentalhealthbench/), [OpenAI paper (PDF)](https://cdn.openai.com/ctf-cdn/MentalHealthBench_A_Comprehensive_Benchmark_of_AI_Capabilities_in_Realistic_Mental_Health_Conversations.pdf))
  <!-- k: t=openai-health,ai-therapy-evidence e=openai,anthropic axis=research-and-evidence -->
- **A Vals AI study of 648 simulated 10-message teen conversations with nine AI models found that 27.5% included a failure on at least one of its most serious safety checks, and that in about 62% of those the failure came later in the exchange rather than in the first reply.** The scenarios were written by five clinicians and the teen's messages were simulated by a model; failures included agreeing to help a teen conceal a suicide attempt (4 of 52 conversations where the check applied), giving a diagnosis (109 of 169) and dropping a referral to a doctor or trusted adult. An instruction telling the chatbot the user is 13 to 17 cut the critical-failure share from 31.1% to 11.5% across 270 conversations each way. Models were tested through their APIs, not consumer apps, per-model rates ranged from 9.7% to 47.2% with overlapping ranges that Vals says show no clear ranking, and grading used AI judges with a clinician-reviewed sample. Vals is a third-party evaluator rather than a model maker, and the study was reported the same day as OpenAI's own benchmark. ([Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/evaluating-ai-safety-in-teen-conversations), [The Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/09/23/chatbots-still-make-risky-errors-talks-with-teens-about-mental-health/))
  <!-- k: t=ai-therapy-evidence axis=research-and-evidence -->
- **The CDC's National Center for Health Statistics reported that the final US suicide rate for 2024 was 13.7 per 100,000, down from 14.1 in 2023, with no significant change since 2017.** The decline was concentrated in women 25 and older and in males 15 to 24 (21.2 to 20.2) and 25 to 44 (29.8 to 28.5); firearms remained the leading means for both sexes with no significant change, state rates ran from 5.7 in the District of Columbia to 29.7 in Alaska, and the rate for females 10 to 14 was 2.3 against 2.1 in 2023. ([CDC NCHS Data Brief 572](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/databriefs/db572.pdf))

- **The FDA granted Breakthrough Device Designation to NET Recovery's transcutaneous alternating-current stimulation device as an aid in reducing stimulant use in adults with cocaine or methamphetamine use disorder, a condition with no FDA-approved drug or cleared device (company announcement, 09-23).** The device is already cleared for opioid-withdrawal symptoms; the designation covers a proposed new indication, buys expedited review and priority status, and does not change clearance requirements. The company says it is still designing a pivotal randomized trial and expects the De Novo pathway, and the stimulant use remains investigational, so this is a regulatory status, not efficacy evidence.
  ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/net-recovery-granted-fda-breakthrough-device-designation-for-stimulant-use-disorder-where-no-drug-is-fda-approved-or-medical-device-fda-cleared-302886966.html), [Psychiatric Times](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/fda-grants-breakthrough-device-designation-to-net-recovery-s-device-for-stimulant-use-disorder))
