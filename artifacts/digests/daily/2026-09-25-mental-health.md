## Policy, regulation & legal

- **TikTok and ByteDance agreed Friday evening to pay Alabama at least $100 million and, for TikTok in Alabama, to give teen users a two-hour default daily limit, midnight-to-6am lockouts and an option to switch to a non-personalized feed, settling the state's teen mental-health lawsuit three days before what would have been the first state jury trial against the platform.** Alabama Attorney General Steve Marshall announced the settlement on 09-25. The consent decree, entered by Montgomery Circuit Judge Monet Gaines without adjudication of any issue and without an admission of wrongdoing (TikTok denies liability in the decree), covers TikTok in Alabama only. Within nine months TikTok must offer teens an option to switch to a non-personalized feed and may not preselect it (the attorney general's release calls it a default; the decree text does not). Within four months it must add "productive pauses" at 60 and 90 minutes of cumulative daily use plus a notice after each 15 minutes of continuous use. The decree also bans cosmetic filters for teens and requires stronger age assurance, parental controls and private-by-default teen accounts, and Alabama releases certain claims under the federal children's-privacy law (COPPA). The money is $116.2 million up front, made up of $100 million in restitution and remediation due within 45 days of the decree's effective date, plus $14.2 million in attorneys' fees and $2 million in litigation costs due within 30, and up to $183.8 million more that Alabama can draw in four tiers as 10, 20, 30 and 40 state attorneys general sign qualifying agreements, each tier paid over ten years; Marshall's office says the total could reach $300 million. A stricter package, including a 10pm-to-7am night mode and a possible 60-minute daily cap, takes effect only if Meta, Snap and YouTube adopt matching terms. Alabama had alleged that TikTok built its endless feed and recommendation algorithm to keep children on the platform, steered some toward self-harm and violence content, and misled parents about safeguards; those allegations were never tried, and TikTok's USDS joint-venture spokesperson said the deal "builds on our commitment and core objective to continually enhance our robust safety tools to protect teens." The Associated Press notes the platform-level changes are roughly similar to those Meta agreed to in its summer settlement with nearly every state, and Alabama would have been the first state to argue its claims against TikTok before a jury; TikTok had argued federal law shields platforms from liability for user-created content.
