## Today's throughline

**The Fed chair used his first Jackson Hole keynote to talk about
artificial intelligence and to refuse, at length and on principle, to say
anything about interest rates — and said nothing at all about the fight
over the Fed's independence.** Kevin Warsh's "In Our Time," delivered at
10:00 ET on his hundredth day as chairman, opens by calling its own
outline "a trail map . . . just don't call it forward guidance," and it
means it: he explicitly declines to offer a reaction function, argues
that forward guidance "has overstayed its welcome" as a routine practice,
and makes the case for "a quieter Fed." On inflation he is direct — 12-month
PCE at **3.7%**, the six-month at **4.1%**, "running above our 2 percent
target," and "otherwise, we have work to do" — while calling labour
markets consistent with full employment. **What the market was actually
positioned for, he did not touch.** The words "independence,"
"administration," "buyback" and "average inflation" do not appear in the
speech at all; the single use of "Treasury" refers to Treasury securities
as a market signal. **This map went into the day with a specific,
logged question — whether the keynote would address the TGA and
long-end mechanism — and the answer is a clean no.** That is a resolved
negative, not a research gap.

## Capital in my markets

- **Warsh put a number on the AI trade from the Fed's own podium, and it
  is a token-revenue number.** In the section of the keynote given to
  financial innovation, he said: *"Reports put annualized token sales for
  the two leading labs alone at more than $100 billion—an increase of
  500-plus percent from a year ago."* He frames AI as **"a new
  variable—potentially a new factor of production"** and says progress
  "has been faster even than its evangelists predicted," describing "a
  kind of hyper–Moore's law" and "a hinge point in history." **The
  questions he then poses are this map's own questions, asked by the
  central bank:** where the returns land and on what timescale — *"how
  much of the surplus goes to owners of scarce assets—AI labs,
  chipmakers, energy producers, and cloud providers?"* — whether token
  usage is complementary or competitive to labour, whether the next model
  generation demands more capital intensity or less, and what the
  equilibrium price of a token even is. ⚠️ **He attributes the $100bn
  figure to "reports," not to Fed data**, and that hedge is his, not
  added here. A task force on productivity and jobs is studying it, and
  he states plainly its recommendations *"have no bearing on decisions we
  make in the current policy conjuncture."*
  ([Federal Reserve, released text](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/warsh20260828a.htm))

- **On rates he gave a doctrine instead of a path, and named the doctrine
  explicitly.** *"I wish our understanding of the economy were so precise
  as to provide a mechanical, tried-and-true answer—that some simple
  function like a Taylor rule could be rigorously relied upon. But our
  knowledge just doesn't extend that far."* He argues forward guidance was
  right for the Global Financial Crisis and has outlived it, that
  "transparency in communications about future policy decisions is not a
  virtue unto itself," and that the Fed needs market signals "as
  unfiltered as possible" — Treasury prices and volumes, the dollar, credit
  cost and availability, commodities — which is an argument for the Fed
  reading markets rather than steering them. **The reaffirmed 2% PCE
  target is the concrete piece for the rates threads:** he treats it as
  firm and fixed, and the phrase "average inflation" appears nowhere,
  which answers a standing question about whether the flexible-average
  framework survives this chairmanship.
  ([Federal Reserve, released text](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/warsh20260828a.htm))
