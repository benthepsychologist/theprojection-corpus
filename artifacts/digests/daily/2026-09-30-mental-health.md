## Today's throughline

California's governor signed two of four AI health-care bills and vetoed the other two, including the one limiting AI in psychotherapy, and the 988 lifeline's LGBTQ+ youth option returned after 14 months. Governor Gavin Newsom signed AB 1979, which bars health facilities from using AI to independently perform licensed clinical functions, and SB 503, on bias in clinical decision-support tools, and vetoed SB 903, which would have barred companion chatbots from delivering psychotherapy, and AB 2575, which would have protected clinicians who override AI recommendations. A Marion County grand jury found Oregon State Hospital short of beds, trained staff and overnight physician coverage on the day Governor Tina Kotek's plan to make it a standalone agency was reported. Portland's council finalized an ordinance making personal psychedelic use a low police priority (10 in favor, two absent), a Brazilian judge ordered Discord to tighten child safeguards after a 13-year-old's suicide, and a 20-patient uncontrolled study presented at the radiation-oncology society's annual meeting reported that incisionless radiosurgery cut depression scores by 53% on average.

The Senate health committee also advanced Timothy Westlake, the nominee to lead federal mental-health and substance-use policy, on a 12-11 party-line vote. Beyond that, no new court or payer action on this lens's open threads turned up through Wednesday night, and none has been reported in Florida's injunction motion against OpenAI (filed Monday 09-28).

- **California Governor Gavin Newsom signed AB 1979 and SB 503 on 09-30, laws that bar health facilities, clinics and physician offices from using AI to independently perform clinical functions that require a professional license and that require developers and users of AI clinical decision-support tools to reduce known bias.** AB 1979 (Bonta, Chapter 854) also tells health facilities, clinics and physician offices to ensure a licensed professional keeps independent judgment when care is informed by such a tool, and deems businesses offering a consumer "health care chatbot" providers of health care under the state's medical-privacy act; SB 503 (Weber Pierson, Chapter 857) requires developers to document intended uses and foreseeable risks and deployers to monitor for biased impacts. ## 📚 Research & evidence

- **A 20-patient uncontrolled study presented at the American Society for Radiation Oncology's annual meeting reported that incisionless stereotactic radiosurgical capsulotomy cut depression scores by 53% on average in treatment-resistant depression, and that thoughts of suicide fell from 14 patients to one (coverage 09-30).** Lead author Mohamed Khattab of the University of South Florida reports that 61% still met the response criteria at the last follow-up, a median of 38.6 months after treatment at Vanderbilt University Medical Center, with no cognitive decline; he and ASTRO's Markus Bredel caution that the study had no comparison group and that larger controlled trials are needed.
  ([Applied Radiology](https://appliedradiology.com/articles/astro-2026-stereotactic-radiosurgery-demonstrates-potential-in-treating-severe-depression), [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/mental-health/focused-brain-radiation-could-ease-severe-tough-to-treat-depression))
  <!-- k: t=neuromodulation-evidence axis=research -->
- **Researchers published the AWARE framework in JMIR Medical Education on 09-30, a five-part set of questions to help clinicians ask patients how they use AI chatbots: how often, why, how attached they feel, risks to their sense of reality, and the effect on daily functioning.** Lead author Alexandre Hudon and colleagues frame it as a structured, nonjudgmental conversation aid rather than a test; the release does not report any validation data.
  ([JMIR Publications via Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/is-your-patient-chatting-with-ai-new-tool-guides-psychiatrists-on-assessing-patients-ai-use))
