# Q8 — Does the screens causality fight resolve? (research memo, 2026-08-07)

Extends `ebp-crawl-digital-ai-science.md` §4 (social media & mental health), which already established the core shape of this fight: Haidt's causal case vs. the Odgers/Przybylski/Vuorre/Ferguson skeptic camp, the 2026 Sigaud/Rausch/McClean/Haidt rebuttal of Vuorre & Przybylski's cross-national null studies, the Sapien Labs correlational counterweight, the school-phone-ban split (academic yes / mental-health no), and Australia's under-16 ban showing ~80% evasion at 3 months. This memo does not re-derive those — it chases what was left open: the rebuttal's reception, Australia's enforcement escalation, US court outcomes, new school-phone-ban evidence, new natural experiments, genuinely new causal designs, and where the youth mental-health trend lines actually sit in 2025-26 data.

Method: WebFetch-first against SAGE/journal pages, Bloomberg Law, Fortune, JURIST, ProMarket, Platformer, CDC, eSafety Commissioner, and Wikipedia's law tracker; 6 WebSearch calls used (rebuttal reception, Australia enforcement, NetChoice litigation, school-phone-ban updates, youth-MH trend data, new causal-design studies). Science.org's "Unsettled science on social media" piece 403'd (paywalled) and is cited via title only; the eSafety Commissioner's own page timed out on fetch, so Australia figures rely on secondary press coverage (Fortune, Gizmodo, JURIST), cross-checked across three independent outlets.

## TL;DR

- **The Sigaud/Rausch/McClean/Haidt rebuttal has no published counter-response yet.** Checked three separate ways (the SAGE journal page itself, a Platformer piece specifically on the Haidt/Odgers fight, and general search) — as of this crawl (Aug 2026), Vuorre, Przybylski, and Odgers have not published a reply to the April 2026 *Clinical Psychological Science* piece. The fight is currently one-sided in publication terms, not resolved.
- **Australia's ban response is escalating penalties, not producing evidence the ban works.** Maximum fines are being doubled (~A$49.5M → ~A$99M, roughly $34M → $68M USD) and the eSafety Commissioner is gaining power to compel internal platform documents, while ~85% of under-16s were still using banned platforms three months in and no wellbeing signal — positive or negative — has been measured yet.
- **US state age-verification law litigation is trending away from a clean industry win.** NetChoice still wins most trial-court rounds (Louisiana permanent injunction Dec 2025, Nebraska blocked June 2026) but appellate courts are increasingly letting laws take effect while cases proceed (Ohio revived by the Sixth Circuit, Florida's injunction stayed by the Eleventh Circuit, Mississippi's law live after SCOTUS declined to block it) — a "judicial sorting process" heading toward Supreme Court review rather than a settled doctrine.
- **School phone bans still show zero mental-health signal in the newest, most rigorous US test.** A preliminary causal (matched-comparison) study using National Survey of Children's Health data across 2016-2024 found no evidence bans reduced screentime or improved any of eight wellbeing measures — extending, not contradicting, the existing NBER/UK null finding.
- **The one genuinely new causal design (a within-person panel study of 25,629 UK adolescents) found no effect** — reinforcing the skeptic side with better methodology than most of the existing literature, while the underlying US youth-depression trend line itself shows tentative signs of turning down for the first time since 2013, per CDC's own trends report — a fact the "crisis is worsening" framing that dominates most 2025-26 secondary coverage doesn't reflect.

## What changed 2021→2026

**The academic fight escalated rather than resolved, and it's currently one-sided.** Section 4 already flagged the Sigaud/Rausch/McClean/Haidt paper (published online April 11, 2026, in *Clinical Psychological Science*) as a direct methodological attack on Vuorre & Przybylski's flagship null-result studies — arguing they blend incompatible technologies/groups together and that one of the three studies involved Meta in the research design. What's new this pass: there is no published reply. Neither the SAGE journal page nor a dedicated Platformer retrospective on the Haidt/Odgers fight shows any rejoinder from Vuorre, Przybylski, or Odgers as of this crawl. Odgers's most recent public position found in this pass — a *Nature* review of *The Anxious Generation* reiterating "the book's repeated suggestion that digital technologies are rewiring our children's brains... is not supported by science," and that her own longitudinal tracking data finds social media "does not emerge as a meaningful predictor of teen mental health" — predates the 2026 rebuttal and doesn't address it. Read plainly: Haidt's camp has thrown the most recent punch, and it is currently unanswered in the literature, which is itself informative for a feed tracking "does this resolve" — it means the debate is not moving toward synthesis, it's moving toward the two camps talking past each other with an unreturned volley sitting on the board.

**Australia's ban response has moved from "will law work" to "how do we make the law bite harder," without new evidence the underlying theory is sound.** Since the June 2026 evasion findings section 4 already captured, the government's answer was not to reassess the policy but to strengthen enforcement: legislation introduced in Parliament in June 2026 doubles the maximum penalty for platform non-compliance from roughly A$49.5 million (~$34M USD) to roughly A$99 million (~$68M USD), and proposes giving the eSafety Commissioner power to compel platforms' internal documents for any enforcement action. The regulator opened investigations into five platforms — Meta's Facebook and Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok, and YouTube — in March 2026 for suspected non-compliance, and an April 2026 compliance review found platforms letting minors repeatedly retry failed age-checks and failing to block under-16 account creation outright. More than 5 million accounts have been deactivated, restricted, or removed since the ban took effect (Dec 10, 2025), yet independent tracking (University of Newcastle, echoed by a subsequent study putting the figure over 85%) still found the large majority of under-16s accessing banned platforms three months in, via fake age claims, VPNs, and simple evasion tricks (e.g., facial-recognition workarounds). No wellbeing/mental-health outcome data — positive or negative — has been published; the government's justification remains an assertion ("necessary to protect children's mental health," per PM Albanese), not a measured result. This is the clearest read available anywhere in this research area on whether a ban law is "fixable rollout friction or fundamentally unworkable" (standing question #3 in the base crawl) — six months in, the answer trending is toward "harder to enforce than modeled," since the government's own pre-launch lab trial had called the age-verification tech "private, robust and effective."

**US litigation went from a NetChoice sweep to a genuine circuit split, with the theory now that this heads to the Supreme Court.** As of this pass: Louisiana's Act 456 is permanently enjoined (Dec 2025, district court summary judgment for NetChoice); Nebraska's age-verification and parental-consent law was blocked days before its July 1, 2026 effective date on First Amendment grounds; Mississippi's law is live in the real world right now — the Fifth Circuit paused the district-court injunction, the Supreme Court declined to intervene at the emergency-relief stage, though Justice Kavanaugh wrote it was "likely unconstitutional," and the Fifth Circuit itself has held two rounds of oral argument (most recently Feb 3, 2026) without ruling; Ohio's under-16 parental-consent law was revived by a fractured three-opinion Sixth Circuit panel in June 2026 after being initially blocked; California's Ninth Circuit mostly upheld the state's ability to enforce its law but struck the specific provision requiring platforms to hide like/share counts from minors by default; Florida's preliminary injunction (won June 2025) was stayed by the Eleventh Circuit in November 2025, and March 2026 oral arguments there showed judges pressing NetChoice on standing rather than accepting a blanket First Amendment theory — described by one legal commentator as a shift from "a clean string of industry wins" to "a judicial sorting process." New suits continue (South Carolina, and Georgia as the eighth state sued). Net trajectory: **trial courts still enjoin nearly every one of these laws on first review; appellate courts are now letting more of them take effect pending appeal than they were a year ago.** Legal observers (e.g., Eric Goldman, quoted in Bloomberg Law coverage) expect the Supreme Court will ultimately have to set the boundary. This is squarely relevant to how Australia's evidence (or lack of it) will be used rhetorically in the US fight — the causal debate and the legal-viability debate are now running on separate, only loosely coupled tracks.

**School-phone-ban evidence gained a new causal design and it still lands null on mental health.** Section 4 already had the NBER working paper and the England 91-school study (real academic-achievement gain, no mental-health gain). New this pass: Henry Saffer's study — described as the first US study to test the causal effect of school phone bans on youth mental health using a matched-comparison design (constructing an untreated comparison group of states whose pre-ban trends match the treated states) — draws on the National Survey of Children's Health, ~18,500 children/year, 2016-2024. It examined eight psychological wellbeing measures and found no evidence bans reduced screentime or improved any of them. The author flags this as preliminary — only three states currently have enough post-ban data to compare — but it's a genuine methodological upgrade on cross-sectional comparisons, and it lands in the same place as the earlier null studies rather than complicating the picture.

**A new set of countries are running the Australia experiment in parallel, at different ages, on different timelines — a real natural-experiment stack building for 2026-2028.** Beyond Australia (age 16, live since Dec 10 2025) and Florida's state-level law (age 14, live since Nov 25 2025), Brazil (age 16) took effect March 17 2026, Indonesia (age 16) March 28 2026, and Malaysia (age 16) June 1 2026 — meaning there are now four to five jurisdictions with under-16-or-14 bans live simultaneously as of this memo's date, each with different enforcement regimes and evasion baselines to compare against Australia's. Scheduled next: Turkey (age 15, passed April 2026, in force ~6 months after publication, i.e. roughly Oct 2026), France (age 15, staged — new accounts from Sept 2026, existing accounts from Jan 2027), Greece (age 15, Jan 2027), the UK (age 16, spring 2027), and the UAE (age 15, expected mid-2027); Denmark has announced an age-15 law with no date set yet. This is the richest natural-experiment calendar the field has ever had — within 18 months there will be comparative evasion-rate and (eventually) outcome data across roughly eight countries with different enforcement mechanisms (facial recognition, ID upload, parental consent), which is exactly the kind of cross-jurisdiction comparison that could start actually adjudicating "is this fixable" rather than relitigating one country's rollout.

**The single strongest new causal-design study to surface in this pass is a within-person panel study, and it lands on the null side.** A random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) — the design explicitly built to separate stable between-person differences from actual within-person change, which is the main methodological weakness Haidt's critics point to in most existing correlational work — was run on 25,629 Greater Manchester adolescents across three annual waves (2021-2023, published 2026 in *Journal of Public Health*, Oxford Academic). It found no evidence that time spent on social media or gaming frequency predicted later internalizing symptoms for either girls or boys, once within-person effects were isolated from between-person correlations (which were small and positive, the standard pattern that gets overinterpreted as causal in cross-sectional work). This is a large-N, purpose-built design directly answering the "genuinely causal new design" ask — and it reinforces the skeptic camp with better methodology than most of the pro-causation literature currently has behind it. Partially offsetting on the pro-causation side: a 2026 instrumental-variable study (using mobile-phone ownership plus home-internet access as an instrument) found evidence that social-media-use-induced by the instrument roughly tripled the odds of adolescent cannabis use — a genuine IV design, but testing a different outcome (substance use, not mental health directly) and not yet mirrored by an IV study on depression/self-harm outcomes specifically.

**The youth mental-health trend data itself is more ambiguous than most coverage suggests, and depends heavily on which dataset you read.** This is the most important correction this pass makes to the standing-question framing. CDC's own official YRBS (Youth Risk Behavior Survey) Data Summary & Trends Report — the field's most authoritative repeated cross-sectional instrument — describes the full 2013-2023 decade as one of rising violence exposure, worsening mental health signs, and rising suicidal thoughts/behaviors, but its most recent read (2021 to 2023) describes "early signs that adolescent mental health is getting better" — a tentative reversal, not a continuation of the "crisis worsening" narrative. This is corroborated by other metrics surfaced in this pass: self-reported major depressive episodes among 12-17-year-olds reportedly peaked at 20.8% in 2021 and fell to 15.4% by 2024; self-harm behavior self-reports rose from 18.0% (2018) to a peak of 21.8% (2022) before easing to 20.2% (2024). Against that, a widely-cited claim that 19.2% of adolescents screened positive for depression in 2025 — "the highest ever recorded" — could not be traced to a primary NCHS source in this pass and should be treated as low-confidence pending verification; it circulates mainly through secondary aggregator sites that also, in the same articles, cite the same CDC 2023 data reframed as "2025 statistics." The UK tells a different story with a more reliable source: NHS England's Mental Health Survey (2025 wave) found 25.8% of 16-24-year-olds affected by a common mental disorder, up from 18.9% in 2014, with 36.1% prevalence among young women — a real, sourced, ongoing worsening trend in the UK even as the tentative US CDC signal points the other way. **Net read: the US youth-mental-health curve may be turning for the first time since 2013, per the government's own trend instrument, while the UK curve is still rising — and neither fact is well reflected in either the pro- or anti-causation camps' current public framing**, both of which tend to treat "the crisis" as a monotonic given. The YRBS is biennial; the 2025 wave was not yet publicly available to this crawl (its release, whenever it lands, is a genuine dated milestone for the feed to watch).

## Current state of the dispute

**Nothing has resolved; the burden of proof has shifted slightly, on both fronts, in ways that cut against Haidt's strongest public framing without vindicating the skeptics either.** On the academic side, the newest, best-designed single study found this pass (the Manchester RI-CLPM, purpose-built to fix the exact methodological complaint skeptics make about the correlational literature) landed null — that's a real point for the "small/no causal effect" camp, not a draw. At the same time, Haidt's camp has an unanswered methodological attack on the skeptics' own flagship cross-national evidence sitting in the literature since April 2026, and their opponents have not yet defended it in print — which means the two camps aren't converging, they're each accumulating separate stacks of unrebutted-by-the-other-side evidence. On the policy side, the "natural experiment" that was supposed to settle the practical question — Australia's ban — is trending toward "enforcement is harder than modeled" (rising fines, expanding investigative powers, still ~85% evasion at 3 months) with zero wellbeing evidence either way, while a wave of comparable bans in other countries (Brazil, Indonesia, Malaysia now; Turkey, France, Greece, UK, UAE on deck through 2027) is about to generate genuinely comparable cross-jurisdiction data for the first time. And underneath all of it, the outcome variable itself — is youth mental health actually getting worse — is more contested and possibly more favorable than either camp's public rhetoric currently assumes, per CDC's own recent-trend read. The honest 2026 state of play is: **more rigorous evidence exists than in 2021, most of the new rigorous evidence points toward smaller/no effects than the strongest causal claims assert, but the policy apparatus (bans, state laws, litigation) is moving on a track that isn't waiting for that evidence to firm up, and won't be forced to reckon with it until the current wave of natural experiments actually reports out over 2026-2028.**

## Feed implications

The feed tracks this as thread `social-media-causality-fight`. Based on this pass, three refinements are worth making to how it's tracked, plus a set of dated expectations to seed the ledger:

**Refinement 1 — split the academic-dispute sub-thread from the policy-natural-experiment sub-thread explicitly.** They are currently running on separate evidentiary tracks (the Manchester within-person null result and the unanswered Sigaud rebuttal are pure-academic; Australia's enforcement escalation and the NetChoice circuit split are pure-policy) and conflating them in one thread risks a policy headline (e.g., "Australia doubles fines") getting read as academic evidence, when it's actually evidence about enforcement difficulty, not about causality.

**Refinement 2 — add a standing sub-question specifically about the youth-MH trend line itself**, separate from the causal-mechanism question: "Is the US CDC YRBS 2021→2023 'early signs of improvement' finding confirmed or reversed by the 2025 wave?" This is a clean, dated, falsifiable tracking item that the base crawl didn't have and that most public discourse currently ignores in either direction.

**Refinement 3 — track the multi-country ban stack as a single comparative natural-experiment cohort**, not one-off national stories. With Brazil/Indonesia/Malaysia now live alongside Australia and Florida, and Turkey/France/Greece/UK/UAE landing through 2027, cross-jurisdiction evasion-rate and (eventually) outcome comparisons become possible for the first time — worth a dedicated comparison table in the thread once 2-3 more countries report their own 3-month evasion data.
