# Q7 — Do DTx solve engagement + payment? (research memo, 2026-08-07)

## TL;DR

- **Engagement is still unsolved.** The newest 2025-2026 literature (Linardon meta-analyses, the npj Digital Medicine engagement review, and a fresh batch of 2026 papers surfaced this pass) shows the same 18-28% attrition band as before, no agreed engagement metric, gamification confirmed not to help, and reminders/human-support the only modest lever — no intervention has actually solved attrition; the field is still describing the problem in new populations, not fixing it.
- **The payment side has two live natural experiments, and both are still in their pre-data ramp-up phase, not producing results.** Germany's DiGA reform legally requires ≥20% of a digital therapeutic's price to be outcomes-linked as of January 1, 2026 — but the performance data that would drive any actual re-pricing isn't due to the regulator (BfArM) until April 15, 2027. **No DiGA product has been re-priced on performance yet.** CMS's Digital Mental Health Treatment (DMHT) HCPCS codes, live since the CY2025 Physician Fee Schedule, still show "low" claims volume per CMS's own acknowledgment, and outside counsel describes the underlying device-payment mechanism (Medicare Administrative Contractor pricing, with no CMS-set national rate) as "opaque, inconsistently applied, and logistically difficult to navigate" — a bureaucratic replay of the exact reimbursement failure that sank Pear Therapeutics, now built into Medicare's own architecture.
- **PHTI itself is the one place a real 2021→2026 shift shows up** — not in a fixed product, but in an institutional thesis that has hardened and generalized. Its July 2026 "Payment for Clinical AI" report extends the identical argument it made about mental-health DTx in 2025 (fee-for-service and capitation both misalign incentives; "autonomous clinical AI requires new payment models, not incremental modifications") to all of clinical AI, using hypertension as the case study — the diagnosis has spread past mental health, even though no commercial cure has emerged anywhere it's been applied.
- **Commercial results in 2026 read as continuation, not resolution.** Akili's OTC pivot (EndeavorOTC) generated real early traction at launch (Q3 2023: $553K revenue, 176,559 downloads, 7,535 paid subscribers at ~$11/month) but total company revenue was still only ~$1.94M trailing-twelve-months as of April 2026, three years post-launch and roughly two years into private ownership under Virtual Therapeutics. Click Therapeutics (Rejoyn's maker) raised a reported $50M Series D around April 2026 alongside reported workforce cuts — capital-raising-plus-cutting is the same pattern that preceded Pear's collapse, now visible again roughly two years after Rejoyn's FDA clearance.
- **A genuine dating discrepancy surfaced in the book's own draft**, worth a fact-check pass: Chapter 1's outline (Scene 4.2) dates PHTI's "Virtual Solutions for Depression and Anxiety" report to **April 2024**, but Chapter 3's outline and this pass's primary-source PDF pull both put its actual cover date at **May 2025** (V1.1 posted June 6, 2025; V1.2 posted September 3, 2025). April 2024 is the correct date for a *different* PHTI report — Digital Diabetes Management — per Chapter 3's own text. See "For the book" below.

## What changed 2021→2026

The arc the book already tells (see "What the book already establishes" below) runs: 2021 money floods in with nobody asking evaluation questions → 2022-23 reckoning (Cerebral's WSJ exposé, Pear's bankruptcy) proves evidence was never the bottleneck → 2023 PHTI is founded as the institutional answer → 2024-25 PHTI starts naming names → 2026 the reform mechanisms Pear's collapse implicitly called for (outcomes-linked pricing, in Germany; digital-benefit reimbursement codes, in the US) go live.

What this research pass adds is where things actually stand *inside* that 2026 moment, on both structural problems Q7 tracks:

**Engagement.** No change in kind, only in documentation breadth. The two anchor meta-analyses (Linardon et al., World Psychiatry 2024, 176 RCTs; the Lancet Digital Health 2025 update) and the npj Digital Medicine 2025 engagement review already established: attrition 18.6% at posttest rising to 28.4% at follow-up, no agreed engagement metric, gamification doesn't help, reminders plus human support does modestly. This pass surfaced four more 2026 papers via PubMed (Tandon et al., JMIR Mental Health — culturally-adapted interventions; Liu et al., JAMA Psychiatry — attrition patterns across RCTs; Jabs et al., Early Intervention in Psychiatry — university-student smartphone interventions; Sinha et al., JMIR Formative Research — Wysa's app-feature/continuity-of-care work). All were read at title/abstract level only via a PubMed search snippet, not independently verified against full text, so treat them as leads rather than confirmed findings — but none of the abstracts surfaced claims of a solved-attrition breakthrough; the newest work is extending the same unsolved-problem finding into new subpopulations, not overturning it.

**Payment.** This is where 2021→2026 shows real, if incomplete, structural movement — three concrete mechanisms now exist that didn't in 2021:
1. **Germany's DiGA outcomes-linked pricing mandate** (live January 1, 2026, ≥20% of reimbursement tied to performance; existing vendor agreements without this component must be renegotiated).
2. **CMS's Digital Mental Health Treatment HCPCS codes** (G0552 device supply/onboarding, contractor-priced; G0553 $20.06 for the first 20 minutes/month of treatment management; G0554 $19.73 per additional 20 minutes — all live since the CY2025 Physician Fee Schedule).
3. **PHTI's own institutional pivot toward prescribing the payment fix directly** rather than only diagnosing it — a "guide to performance-based contracting for digital health solutions" was reported (via secondary coverage, not independently confirmed by direct fetch) as expected in early 2026, and the July 2026 "Payment for Clinical AI" report makes the payment-model argument PHTI's headline institutional thesis rather than a footnote inside a condition-specific report.

None of the three has yet produced an observable outcome (a re-priced product, a claims-volume inflection, a documented procurement shift). All three are real, dated, checkable commitments — which is itself the useful finding: the "reform" layer has moved from advocacy to mechanism, but the mechanism hasn't produced a result yet as of this writing.

## Current state of the dispute

**Does DTx solve the engagement problem?** No. The 2026 literature keeps confirming the same numbers rather than reporting a fix. This is the more settled of the two structural problems in the sense that there's no active argument about whether it's solved — everyone agrees it isn't; the open question is whether it's solvable at all with current app-based delivery models, or whether it's structural to unsupervised self-directed digital care (see Strand 1/5 of the companion EBP crawl for the "digital placebo" and passive-sensing angles feeding into this).

**Does DTx solve the payment paradox?** Actively contested, and the contest is happening on schedule rather than resolving. Three things are true simultaneously: (1) the mechanisms that are supposed to fix it are real and dated — DiGA's ≥20% performance-linked pricing is now law, not aspiration; CMS's DMHT codes are live, not proposed; (2) neither mechanism has produced a result yet — DiGA's first performance-data delivery to BfArM isn't due until April 15, 2027, so **no DiGA product has actually been re-priced on outcomes yet**, and CMS's own rulemaking record acknowledges DMHT claims volume remains low two years after the codes went live; (3) the specific failure mode each mechanism is running into is a *procurement/administrative* one, not a *clinical-evidence* one — CMS never set a national payment rate for the DTx device itself, punting to opaque, inconsistent Medicare Administrative Contractor pricing, which is functionally the same "the money side, not the evidence, is broken" failure that killed Pear. The paradox isn't resolved; it has relocated one layer down, from "will payers reimburse this at all" (2017-2023) to "does the reimbursement mechanism actually function once it exists on paper" (2024-2026).

**Vendor-level dispute.** PHTI's flagship mental-health assessment ("Virtual Solutions for Depression and Anxiety," cover-dated May 2025) has already been through two vendor-driven corrections: V1.1 (posted June 6, 2025) added detail on Lyra Health's solution-specific economic analysis and corrected the report to reflect that **Lyra does offer performance guarantees** — a direct, substantive walk-back of an earlier characterization; V1.2 (posted September 3, 2025) corrected underlying data from a cited study (Hall 2024), stated not to change the report's findings. This is exactly the shape of dispute the book's Chapter 3 outline flags as "Lyra Health's October 2025 methodology-contest letter" — but I was **not able to independently corroborate a specific October 2025 letter** in this pass (WebSearch budget for the task was exhausted before a direct hit; several targeted searches and search-engine WebFetches turned up nothing under that description). What I can independently confirm is the surrounding pattern: PHTI's report underwent two vendor-attributable corrections in 2025, Aon (a benefits consultant, not PHTI) has separately published its own "Lyra Health Cost Efficiency Analysis" claims studies that PHTI's report cites as evidence, and PHTI's own methodology page describes no formal vendor dispute/appeal process — meaning any "contest" a vendor mounts would necessarily take the informal form of a letter or public pushback rather than a structured review, consistent with what the book's outline describes. Flag this to the book's fact-check pass rather than treating it as independently reconfirmed here.
