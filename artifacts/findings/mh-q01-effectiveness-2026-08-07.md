# Q1 — Is care getting more effective, or just more available? (research memo, 2026-08-07)

Extends `ebp-crawl-therapy-science.md` §1 (psychotherapy outcomes) and §5 (what works at scale). This memo does not re-derive those findings — it chases what they left open: era-moderator/time-trend evidence, reach-expansion numbers, the treatment-gap stat's movement, LMIC evidence maturation, and whether harms/deterioration measurement is becoming routine.

Method: WebFetch-first against PubMed/PMC/journal pages/WHO/NHS/lab sites; 6 WebSearch calls used (Johnsen–Friborg/Cuijpers time-trend debate; Schleider lab state rollout; treatment-gap trend; task-sharing scale-up numbers; Montana SSI rollout; CONSORT harms-reporting trend). Several primary sources (Wiley, some PMC search interfaces) 402/403/404'd; noted inline where a claim rests on secondary coverage instead of primary text.

---

## TL;DR

- **No evidence absolute response/recovery rates are climbing over time.** The one direct test of this — Johnsen & Friborg's 2015 claim that CBT's effect on depression is *falling* year over year — was reanalyzed by Cristea/Cuijpers with 30 additional RCTs and found to be a statistical artifact (the "decline" only showed up in US-only subsamples and vanished in most multivariate models). Net verdict from the one era-moderator fight that's actually been run: **flat**, not falling, not rising.
- **Reach has scaled far faster than outcomes have moved.** NHS Talking Therapies grew from 533,550 referrals (2011/12) to 670,000 people treated (2023/24) — roughly 5x volume — while its headline recovery rate has sat in the same low-to-mid-40s-to-50% band the entire time (43% in 2012/13, ~50% in 2024/25). Task-sharing programs outside the UK now reach tens of millions directly (StrongMinds 1.3M treated, Healthy Entrepreneurs 18M reached, WorldBeing 4M students via 355,000 trained teachers) — a genuine scale story, not an effectiveness one.
- **The treatment gap is still enormous, and this session could not verify whether it has actually narrowed since the field's well-known circa-2004 baseline estimates.** Current WHO figures (Sept 2025): fewer than 10% of people with mental disorders in low-income countries receive care vs. over 50% in high-income countries; median government mental-health spending has been flat at ~2% of health budgets since 2017 — i.e., the resourcing lever that would close the gap hasn't moved either.
- **Deterioration/harms reporting just crossed from optional to mandatory at the guideline level.** CONSORT 2025 folded the previously-separate CONSORT-Harms extension directly into the core trial-reporting checklist (new required items on how harms were defined/assessed, concomitant care, benefits-and-harms objectives) — a structural change, though enforcement/compliance is untested. A 2025 review of psilocybin-therapy trials independently found reporting quality rising with time: all six trials rated "high quality" on side-effect reporting were published 2020 or later.
- **Single-session interventions (SSIs) are the one genuinely new delivery mechanism since 2021, and they just left the research-prototype stage.** Montana became the first US state (announced April 2025) to fund Schleider lab's Project YES as a free, statewide, HRSA-funded public service — a jump from academic RCT to government infrastructure — but the underlying effect (pooled SMD −0.25, fading by one year) is unchanged and modest; it's a reach innovation riding on an already-known, limited effect size.

## What changed 2021→2026

**The "are effects declining" question got its one real empirical test, and it resolved toward "no."** This wasn't previously covered in the base survey. Johnsen & Friborg (2015, *Psychological Bulletin*) ran the closest thing the field has to an era-moderator meta-analysis: 70 CBT-for-depression trials from 1977–2014, regressing effect size on publication year, and reported a significant decline. Cristea, Cuijpers and colleagues revisited it (VU Amsterdam-published revision, cited widely through 2024–2025 discourse) using RCTs only, adding 30 more eligible trials the original search missed. Their multiple-regression models found publication year was either non-significant, or significant only in combination with country — and the "decline" was present *solely* in US-based studies, disappearing once geography was controlled for. Their stated conclusion: year of publication "does not appear to be a reliable and independent moderator" of CBT's effectiveness for depression. This is the field's one substantive answer to "is anything getting more effective over time" and the answer is: not detectably, once methodology is tightened. No comparable era-moderator meta-analysis was found for psychotherapy broadly (only this CBT-specific fight); Cuijpers' own group has not, as far as this search could determine, published a dedicated time-trend/era-moderator analysis outside this one reanalysis.

**Reach-expansion numbers are now large enough to quantify the "more available" side of the question precisely.** NHS Talking Therapies (formerly IAPT) trend, assembled from NHS Digital/Wikipedia historical reporting: 533,550 referrals in 2011/12 (~60% entering treatment); 761,848 referrals in 2012/13 (49% entering treatment, 43% recovery among completers); nearly 1.3 million referrals by 2014/15 (815,665 entered treatment, only ~22% of those who entered showed reliable recovery); 670,000 people treated in 2023/24 with the service now reporting ~50% recovery / two-thirds "improved" (per the 2024/25 annual report already in the base survey). Read across the full span, the volume line goes up roughly 5x while the recovery-rate line oscillates in a ~20-point band that never establishes a clear upward trend — exactly the "flat outcome under scaled reach" pattern the standing question asks about. Task-sharing outside the UK has independently become a quantifiable, not just anecdotal, scale story since 2021: StrongMinds reports 1.3 million people treated for depression (as of Feb 2026); Healthy Entrepreneurs has trained 20,000+ community health entrepreneurs across seven countries reaching 18 million people (by 2025); WorldBeing has trained 355,000 teachers reaching 4 million students; Lagos State's MeHPriC program trained 852 primary health care workers across 57 facilities in a single 2021 push (surpassing all prior Nigerian mhGAP scale-ups combined) with knowledge gains sustained at 5 months (Cohen's d=0.97). McKinsey's 2025/2026 analysis frames the ceiling: task-sharing could in principle reach 660 million people with untreated mild-to-moderate conditions and prevent 18.6 million healthy-life-years lost, but "few mental health task-sharing programs have reached wide-scale adoption" due to fragmented, short-term funding — the scale-up is real but still small relative to need.

**Single-session interventions crossed from academic pilot to state government infrastructure.** Montana's April 2025 announcement — first-in-the-nation, HRSA-funded, delivered through the state's Access to Pediatric Psychiatry Network, explicitly designed around rural Montana kids living 200+ miles from a provider — is a genuine 2021→2026 change: SSIs went from "Schleider lab RCT" (the base survey's SMD −0.25 pooled effect) to a live public-service deployment mechanism. This is a reach/access innovation, not an effectiveness one — the underlying per-person effect size is unchanged and still fades by one year per the existing umbrella review.

**Harms/deterioration reporting moved from "chronically ignored, optional extension" toward "mandatory core requirement" — a structural, guideline-level change.** CONSORT 2025 (the master trial-reporting standard nearly all RCTs are held to) integrated the previously-separate CONSORT-Harms extension directly into its core checklist: new required items on how harms were defined and assessed, on reporting concomitant care per group, and on stating benefits-*and*-harms objectives up front — harms reporting is no longer an opt-in extension. Independent evidence that this is translating into practice, not just guideline text: a 2025 systematic review of 24 psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy trials (2005–2024, 917 participants) found reporting quality rising over time — all six trials rated "high quality" on side-effect reporting were published in 2020 or later, even though median adherence across the full 20-year span was only 50% and all RCTs still carried high risk of bias on side-effect outcomes specifically. Read together with the base survey's finding that only 30% of psychotherapy RCT publications even mention negative outcomes, the honest picture is: the *rule* changed in 2025, the *practice* was already trending better before the rule did, but compliance is still well under half even in the most recently studied treatment class.

---

## Current state of the dispute

The reach-vs-effectiveness question does not currently have a clean resolution, and this research pass did not find one hiding in unexamined literature — it found instead that **almost nobody has run the direct test**. The single closest empirical attempt (Johnsen & Friborg's era-moderator meta-analysis) was specific to one modality (CBT) and one disorder (depression), and its "decline" finding didn't survive reanalysis with a larger, RCT-only sample. That leaves the field without a positive claim in either direction at the level of rigor a meta-analysis would provide — nobody has shown response rates rising, and the one paper that claimed they were falling was substantially undermined.

What the evidence *does* establish cleanly is asymmetry: reach numbers are large, quantifiable, and rising steeply (NHS volume 5x in a decade; task-sharing programs individually reaching millions to tens of millions; SSIs now state-funded infrastructure) while outcome numbers — recovery rates, response rates, remission rates — sit in the same bands they've occupied for years across every delivery model examined (NHS ~43-50% recovery since 2012; Cuijpers' 2024 umbrella review's 24-42% response rates by disorder). The treatment gap itself remains wide by every current measure (WHO 2025: <10% LMIC coverage, >50% HIC coverage, spending flat at 2% of health budgets since 2017) but this research pass could not locate a reliable same-methodology comparison to the field's older baseline estimates to state a *trend* on the gap itself with confidence — that is a real, flagged gap in the evidence base as searched, not a finding that the gap is static. On harms, the dispute has essentially been settled in one direction — from "optional/ignored" toward "formally mandatory" — via CONSORT 2025, though whether journals and authors actually comply is an open empirical question the guideline change doesn't answer by itself.

## Feed implications

**Candidate watchlist terms:**
- **"era-moderator meta-analysis"** — why: this is the specific, narrow research design that would settle "is care getting more effective," and this session found only one has ever been run (CBT-only, and its finding didn't hold up); worth a standing search term to catch the next one, in any modality.
- **"CONSORT Harms" / "CONSORT 2025"** — why: the reporting-standard shift is recent (2025) and enforcement/compliance is exactly the kind of thing that shows up gradually in trade coverage and follow-up audits over the next 1–2 years.
- **"Project YES" / "single-session intervention statewide"** — why: Montana is a first-of-its-kind deployment; whether other states follow (or whether Montana's own outcomes get published) is a trackable, discrete event, not a diffuse trend.
- **"task-sharing wide-scale adoption"** — why: McKinsey's own framing ("few programs have reached wide-scale adoption") names the exact bottleneck (fragmented short-term funding) that would need to break for the "more available" story to keep accelerating.

**Thread candidates:**
- **Does Montana's Project YES rollout produce publishable population-level outcome data, and do other states follow?** Why: this is the single most concrete "reach expansion" event from this whole memo — a live natural experiment in exactly the question the feed is asking, with a plausible 12–24 month horizon for first results.
- **Does CONSORT 2025's harms-mandate produce a measurable rise in harms-reporting compliance across psychotherapy (not just psychedelic) trials?** Why: the psilocybin-specific review already shows a pre-mandate improving trend (all high-quality studies since 2020); a 2027–2028 follow-up review would show whether the guideline change accelerated or merely rode an existing curve.
- **Does anyone run a broader (non-CBT-only) era-moderator meta-analysis in the next few years?** Why: the field's evidentiary gap on "are response rates changing over time" is real and specific enough that a single well-powered paper could become the anchor citation the way Cuijpers 2024's response-rate umbrella review already is.

**Dated expectations (each falsifiable on a horizon):**
- **By end of 2027, expect no field-wide (non-CBT-specific) era-moderator meta-analysis to have been published showing a directional trend in absolute response/recovery rates** — why: no group appears to be actively working this question at scale right now (Cuijpers' own 2026 review didn't attempt one), so the evidentiary gap identified here is likely to persist absent a new entrant.
- **By 2027/28, expect at least one other US state to announce a Montana-style statewide SSI deployment** — why: Schleider explicitly "consults for national and state-level health care organizations" per this session's search results, suggesting Montana is a template being actively shopped, not a one-off.
- **By the 2025/26 NHS Talking Therapies annual report (expected mid-2027), expect the recovery rate to remain within the ~45-52% band it has held since roughly 2018** — why: five separate years of data reviewed here show no structural break in that band despite continuous volume growth, so continuity is the base-rate prediction; a genuine break either direction would be a real finding worth flagging.
